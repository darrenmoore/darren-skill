# Playbook: principles, analogies, maxims

Reference for the `marketing-mentor` skill. Pull the one principle, analogy or maxim that fits
the moment. Don't paste this into a reply.

These are paraphrased principles drawn from themes in Chris Zukowski's published writing at
https://howtomarketagame.com, plus the skill's own analogies. None of it is a quote, and none
of it is endorsed by him. Don't attribute wording here to him in public copy; read his site for
the real thing and to cite it.

Many tactics are Steam-native. If the game isn't on Steam, use the translation table in
`SKILL.md`: translate the thinking, never borrow the numbers.

## Core concepts

### 1. Genre is the biggest marketing decision
Choosing what kind of game to make is the biggest marketing decision, made long before anyone
"starts marketing". Apply: establish the genre and judge whether its audience exists and wants
this. Fish in that genre's pools, not generic "indie game" ones.

### 2. The feather and the bowling ball
Some games are bowling balls: push a post and it rolls a foot and stops. Others are feathers that
lift on their own. Apply: hunt for the hook people repeat for you, the visual or one-line idea.
If every post lands with a thud, find the feather before paying for reach.

### 3. Clarity over cleverness
The page should look so obviously like its genre that a shopper thinks "that kind of game, I'm
in." Apply: cut vague, artsy descriptions. Title, hero image and first sentence say what the
player does within seconds.

### 4. Direct-to-player beats borrowed reach
Platforms flare up and die. Owned channels (email, community) build something durable. Apply:
steer effort toward the list, the community and page conversion, away from chasing a feed.

### 5. The page going public is the announcement
The day the store or landing page is live, the game is announced. Apply: don't publish a
half-built page "to iterate later". Have the hero image, gameplay clip and signup capture ready.

### 6. Stairstep
Do only the marketing the current scale has earned. Apply: work out which rung they're on (no
page / page but no list / small list / first community traction) and prescribe that rung's move.
This is the antidote to "do everything everywhere".

### 7. Don't waste the big debut
Don't show an unproven demo at the single biggest visibility moment; one game-breaking bug in
front of the crowd wastes it. Apply: soft-launch to a small group, fix the worst onboarding
drop-off, build the list, then make the big push.

### 8. Small games before huge ones
Build smaller, sustainable wins and learn the platform before betting the runway on a dream
project. Apply: pressure-test scope against team size and runway, and argue for scoping down if
it won't ship.

### 9. Key art is a conversion lever
A screenshot with the title typed over it costs clicks. Apply: the capsule, hero image or store
icon decides whether a cold visitor clicks. Treat it as a deliberate, ideally paid-for, asset.

### 10. Visual genre signals
Genre shorthand in the art does silent work: a hammer says builder, a sword and sail says naval
war. Apply: check colours, icons and lighting for "does a stranger match this to a genre they
already want?"

### 11. Platforms reward conversion, not traffic
Stores amplify pages that turn visitors into wishlists or sales. The same logic holds for ads and
search. Apply: fix page conversion before buying or chasing traffic, or you pay to prove nobody
converts.

## Analogies (quick reference)

- **Skis in the desert**: selling a genre to people who don't want it. For genre and audience
  mismatch.
- **Feather and bowling ball**: a hook people share versus a game nobody reposts.
- **The list doesn't spoil**: wishlists and signups keep, so put the page up and start capturing
  early.
- **Key art is click-through rate**: the image is the conversion lever, not decoration.

## Maxims

1. Genre first. Pick one and commit; the market decides, not your passion.
2. Make a good game lots of people already want. Marketing multiplies it; it can't fix bad fit.
3. Put the page up and start the list the day you go public.
4. Don't chase press. Build a community and a list you own.
5. Do only the marketing your current scale has earned.
6. Key art and a gameplay clip beat any amount of posting.
7. Gameplay in the first seconds, lore in the footnotes.
8. If a stranger can't name your genre at a glance, the page is broken.
9. You can't manufacture the magic, so nail everything you control.
10. Decide from real data, never invented data, and still prepare for the worst case.
